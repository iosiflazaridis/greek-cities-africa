How Greek came to Africa, and when it faded

The Patriarchate that outlived the cities

Greek speech left the cities within about two centuries of the Arab conquest. The church did not leave. The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Alexandria lived through centuries in which its flock spoke Arabic and its patriarchs often resided in Constantinople. It came back to Alexandria in 1846/47, as Greek merchants settled in Egypt, and from the 1920s it took in the Orthodox of the whole continent; its patriarch is styled Pope and Patriarch of Alexandria and All Africa. Its rank is ancient, set by the ecumenical councils: at Nicaea (325) it stood with Rome and Antioch at the head of the church, second after Rome, and from the councils of Constantinople (381) and Chalcedon (451) it has ranked second among the sees of the East, after Constantinople, the place it still holds among the Orthodox churches.

Carthage and ancient DNA

Carthage is not counted here as a Greek or bilingual city. Its people spoke Punic, and later Latin. Greek residents are recorded in 396 BC, and Greek monks and refugees in the 7th century AD, but Greek was never one of the city’s main languages.

Ancient DNA adds evidence of another kind. Ringbauer and colleagues (Nature, 2025) studied genomes from Punic sites across the Mediterranean, among them 17 people from Carthage and 27 from Kerkouane on Cape Bon. Most of their ancestry came from a genetic profile like that of Sicily and the Aegean: 84% of the people of Carthage and Kerkouane had more than half of this Sicilian–Aegean ancestry. North African ancestry was a minority, though large in some individuals, and ancestry from the Levant, the Phoenician homeland, was almost absent.

The genomes cannot tell Sicily and the Aegean apart. The authors name contact with the Greek colonies of the central Mediterranean and of Cyrenaica as one possible source. An earlier study of Kerkouane (Moots and colleagues, Nature Ecology and Evolution, 2023) had already found people there whose ancestry resembled that of present-day Italians and Greeks, buried beside people of North African ancestry.

Ancestry is not language. These were Carthaginians in culture, who wrote Punic. The genomes show how mixed the Punic world was, and how much of it came from the Greek side of the sea. They do not make Carthage a Greek city, but raise the possibility that Greeks were among its inhabitants, perhaps adopting the language and culture of the city: an episode of linguistic conversion either in Africa or in the source of these genetically Aegean–Sicilian people.

How it was made

What counts as a Greek city

A self-governing city, a royal or Roman foundation, a nome capital or a bishop’s see, not a camp, fort, sanctuary or trading post, whose residents spoke Greek. The evidence: a polis in the Inventory of Archaic and Classical Poleis, a foundation by a Hellenistic king or a Roman emperor, Greek civic institutions, the metropolis of a nome, a city in Hierocles’ list (c. 527), or a see with a bishop at the councils. In every case there must be positive evidence of resident Greek speakers: officials, a garrison, a court, and Greek used only in the liturgy or in learning do not count. The site must lie in present-day Africa; Egypt includes Sinai.

Greek from

When the place became Greek: its foundation for a Greek foundation; otherwise the first evidence of Greek-speaking residents. A refoundation on the same site keeps the earlier date (Ptolemais, the harbour of Barke). For 26 nome capitals with no earlier evidence of their own, the date is AD 4/5, when Rome registered the gymnasial class of the nome capitals; many of them surely had Greek settlers under the Ptolemies, but it is not documented town by town, so these dates are floors, shown as “by AD 5” and hatched in the timelines.

Greek until

Bilingual cities

A bilingual city had many Greek-speaking residents, but its own language lived on beside Greek. That is the rule for the nome capitals of Egypt, where Egyptian, written in Demotic and then in Coptic, remained the language of most townspeople; Greek was the language of government, of the gymnasium and of many households. Cirta kept Punic and Libyan, and Pharan Arabic. A bilingual city has its own colour, is never said to have become Greek, and its dates are when Greek was spoken there. Four Greek cities had a bilingual period (†): Naukratis, Antinoopolis, Ptolemais Hermiou and Berenike on the Red Sea.

Today

The town on the site is the one whose built-up area covers the ancient city; ruins outside a town count as ruins. A governorate capital of Egypt, or a district seat of eastern Libya, counts if it stands within about 2 km of the city or bears its name. Names are traced through Coptic, Arabic or Latin.

Research

Eight research batches by region (the western and eastern Delta, Middle and Upper Egypt, the Red Sea and the oases, Cyrenaica, the Maghreb, Nubia), then four checks: the start dates, the end dates, the edge cases, and completeness against Hierocles’ list, the nome capitals, the lists of bishops and the royal foundations. The bishops of the Delta sees follow Worp’s checklist of the bishops of Egypt (1994), and the identifiers the Pleiades gazetteer. Every record keeps its sources and notes; the places considered and left out are listed with a reason. Each city’s “Who ended it” line names the act and the people closest to the end of Greek there; no ban on Greek speech is recorded anywhere.
